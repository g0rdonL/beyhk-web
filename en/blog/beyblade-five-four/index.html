Beyblade X models: complete specs, parts and design guide

2026-11-17 · BeyHK

Model codes are the most underrated entry skill in Beyblade X: one string already tells you which product line a top belongs to, which parts it carries, how tall it sits, and what its bit does, before you spend a single dollar. This complete guide to Beyblade X models breaks down the naming rules behind the BX, UX and CX lines from zero, explains what actually differs between a Starter, a Booster, a Random Booster and a boxed set, walks through official part stats, and ends with a situation-based decision framework. After reading it, you can pick up any model code, from BX-54 to CX-20, and say exactly what is in the box and whether it is worth buying.

Key takeaways

  • Every Beyblade X top is assembled from three parts: the Blade, the Ratchet and the Bit. Swap any one of them and performance changes immediately.
  • The toyline splits into three product lines: BX (Basic), UX (Unique) and CX (Custom). A CX blade can be disassembled into four-plus layers, making it the most tunable structure in the system.
  • A Starter (from ¥1,980) includes a launcher; a Booster (¥1,400) is the top only; a Random Booster (¥1,400) is one randomly selected top per box.
  • Model codes state the specs directly: 3-60F means 3 lock points, 6.0 mm tall, Flat bit; 0-70LP means 0 lock points, 7.0 mm tall, and a mode-switching LP bit.
  • The newest BX model is BX-54 Sieg Superion 0-70LP, released on 21 November 2026 at an official price of ¥1,600.

What is a Beyblade X model?

A Beyblade X model is the official product unit of the system: one model code identifies the product line, the top's name and its exact part combination. Every top consists of a Blade, a Ratchet and a Bit, and swapping any component changes performance on the spot, a design Takara Tomy formalises as the Gear structure. That modular design is what separates Beyblade X from every previous generation: you do not buy a finished top, you buy a configuration.

Takara Tomy positions three product lines side by side: BX is the standard Basic line, UX carries the radical shapes and gimmicks, and CX is the fully disassemblable Custom line. Parts interoperate at the Ratchet and Bit level across the lines, but each line's blade follows its own structural logic. The sections below take them one at a time.

How to read a model code

A full code such as "BX-54 Sieg Superion 0-70LP" carries four separate pieces of information: the prefix names the product line, the number is the release serial, the middle code specifies the Ratchet, and the trailing letters specify the Bit. Here is each piece.

The prefix: what BX, UX and CX mean

Prefix Product line Blade structure Positioning
BX Basic One-piece blade, metal and resin balanced Standard three-part system, widest selection, the beginner's starting point
UX Unique Expand Blade: the Ratchet is integrated into the blade Radical shapes and special gimmicks
CX Custom Blade disassembles into four-plus layers (Lock Chip, Main Blade, Assist Blade, metal and Over layers) Highest customisation freedom

The prefix tells you what structure you are buying: BX is the orthodox three-part system; UX merges the Ratchet into the blade to push metal toward the blade's perimeter; CX goes the opposite way and splits the blade into layers so one top can be built several ways.

Product types: Starter, Booster, Random Booster and sets

Within any line, the product type decides what is in the box. The official product list sorts items into Starters, Boosters, Random Boosters, sets and tools, with prices running from ¥700 tools up to ¥8,200 stadium sets. Checking the type before buying is the first step to never buying the wrong thing.

Product type What is in the box Official price Best for
Starter Top + launcher ¥1,980 to ¥2,420 First purchase
Booster Top only ¥1,400 Players who already own a launcher
Random Booster One random top (one of several per box) ¥1,400 Cheap part hunting

Source: Takara Tomy official product list, checked 16 November 2026.

Note where Starter prices vary: a standard BX Starter is usually ¥1,980 while complex configurations reach ¥2,420. Boosters hold at ¥1,400, so buying tops as Boosters and adding launchers separately is the cheaper long-term path once you own your first launcher.

Reading the part code: 0-70LP as the example

The Ratchet code starts with the number of lock points, followed by the height in tenths of a millimetre: 3-60 means 3 lock points at 6.0 mm; 0-70 means 0 lock points at 7.0 mm. More lock points generally means better burst resistance; more height means the contact point sits higher against an opponent.

The trailing letters name the Bit and its movement: F (Flat) is an aggressive flat tip, T (Taper) a slanted one, B (Ball) a stable round tip, N (Needle) a stamina tip. Take BX-54 Sieg Superion 0-70LP: LP is a balance bit that changes its movement depending on how you launch it, with official stats of 30 attack, 15 defence, 30 stamina, 25 dash and 80 burst resistance. Paired with a blade built to attack and defend equally well, the whole top switches between fierce offence and solid defence.

The three product lines: specs and design focus

BX Basic line: metal launcher hook, balanced and durable

BX is built around stability: the launcher hook is metal, weight distribution stays even, and the blade's metal concentrates near the centre for high rigidity. The three-part structure is the most readable: the Blade decides the contact face, the Ratchet decides height and lock points, the Bit decides movement and stamina. Limited reissues run through this line too, such as the BX-48 metal-coat revival box, which carries both collector and competitive value.

UX Unique line: the Ratchet moves into the blade

UX's signature is the Expand Blade system: to push metal toward the blade's outer edge, Takara Tomy merged the Ratchet into the blade, freeing space for radical shapes and gimmicks. The trade-off is that the Ratchet is no longer a separate part, so changing height means changing the whole blade unit. The UX-10 customise set is the line's showcase product: several tops plus launchers in one box, built for tuning and deck battles.

CX Custom line: a blade that disassembles into layers

CX splits the blade into a Lock Chip, a Main Blade and an Assist Blade, adds metal and Over layers, reassembles them into one complete blade unit, and only then attaches the Ratchet and Bit. Take CX-20-01 Phoenix Warden WH: one top consists of the Lock Chip, Main Blade, Assist Blade, Metal Blade, Over Blade, core Ratchet and Bit, and every layer swaps independently. That structure lets one CX top serve as three different builds, which makes CX the most customisable line in the system today.

From the first generation to the fourth

Beyblade X is the franchise's fourth generation: the 1999 original established the modding culture, the 2008 Metal Fight era made metal contact the core, the 2015 Burst era added the burst gimmick, and the X era, launched on 15 July 2023, redefined speed with Xtreme Dash, where the bit's gear bites into the stadium's rail. Understanding that four-generation arc explains why X-era parts obsess over gear bite, height and contact faces: the entire battle system runs on those three things.

Reading model specs: stats, height and spin direction

Every part carries official stats: attack, defence and stamina as the base three, the Bit adds dash and burst resistance, the Ratchet adds height. On BX-54, the blade sits at 38 attack, 35 defence and 27 stamina, the 0-70 Ratchet at 70 height, the LP bit at 80 burst resistance; the three stacked together are the top's profile. Stats are not a strength ranking, they are a matching tool: pick a high-stamina bit for endurance play, check burst resistance and lock-point count when you need to survive upper attacks.

Spin direction is part of the spec sheet too: most blades spin right, a handful of special models spin left, and a left-spin against a right-spin contact behaves completely differently from a right-on-right clash, so deck builders track spin matchup as carefully as type.

Which model should you buy? A decision framework

The short answer: first-time players buy a BX Starter; tuners buy Boosters and part out; players assembling a three-top deck cheaply use Random Boosters; only a full set gives you a complete battle environment. Match your situation:

Situation Best pick Why
First contact with Beyblade X BX Starter (from ¥1,980) Top and launcher included, battle-ready out of the box
Own a launcher, want to tune seriously Booster (¥1,400) Buy the top alone, parts go straight into the deck
Assembling a three-top deck cheaply Random Booster (¥1,400) One random top per box at the lowest entry price
Need a complete battle environment Set (from ¥3,960) Stadium, tops and launchers in one purchase

One deck strategy matters more than any single purchase: competitive decks run three tops, so spread their part combinations instead of stacking all three on the same parts, or one opposing playstyle defeats the whole deck. Random Boosters are a legitimate part source here: the CX-17 box holds eight tops with published pull rates, and it works better as targeted part hunting than as a source of prize tops. How to start with your first model

  1. Pick a BX Starter as your base: top and launcher included, simple code to read, learn the launch first.
  2. Add a proper stadium: official X stadiums carry the accel rail, and a flat surface will never trigger Xtreme Dash.
  3. Learn the basic launch: hold the launcher level over the stadium, pull the short cord once, practise flat shots before chasing rail bites.
  4. Record every battle: log the configuration and the result, wins and losses both, so you know which part to replace next.

Frequently asked questions

What is BX-54?

BX-54 Sieg Superion 0-70LP is a Booster released on 21 November 2026 at an official price of ¥1,600. It is a balance top that switches between offence and defence: the upper blade surface slopes upward for strong attacks, the lower counter blade bounces low opponents away, and the LP bit changes movement depending on how you launch.

Should you buy a Starter or a Booster?

Buy a Starter if you own no launcher: from ¥1,980 you get the top and launcher together. Buy a Booster at ¥1,400 if you already have a launcher and want the top for parts. The arithmetic is simple: two Starters cost about as much as one Starter plus three Boosters, which is far better part value.

What do the two middle numbers in a model code mean?

They are the Ratchet specs: the first number is the lock-point count and the next two give the height in tenths of a millimetre. 3-60 means 3 lock points at 6.0 mm, 0-70 means 0 lock points at 7.0 mm, and M-85 is a large 8.5 mm Ratchet. These numbers directly shape collision height and burst resistance.

How many parts can one model break into?

BX and UX models are three parts: Blade, Ratchet and Bit; UX's Expand Blade merges the Ratchet into the blade, leaving two separate components. CX goes furthest and reaches six: the Lock Chip, Main Blade, Assist Blade, metal layer and Over layer assemble into the blade unit, which then takes the Ratchet and Bit, every layer swappable on its own.

Are Random Boosters worth buying for deck building?

Worth it for parts, not for mains. One box costs ¥1,400 and contains one random top with officially published pull rates, so you are hunting a specific part rather than the prize top. Deck part needs are precise, which makes Random Boosters efficient gap fillers, but core deck tops should come from Starters or Boosters bought directly.

Is buying Beyblade X models in Hong Kong expensive?

Japanese official pricing is the baseline: Starters from ¥1,980, Boosters and Random Boosters at ¥1,400. Hong Kong retail prices shift with import channels, exchange rates and shop markup, popular models and limited runs regularly sell above list, so compare two or three shops before ordering and check whether you are buying the Asian release or the Japanese one.
